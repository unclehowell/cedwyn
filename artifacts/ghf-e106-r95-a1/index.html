1) INTRODUCTION AND ACKNOWLEDGEMENTS

Following the demolition of Great House Farm, Llandough in December 1988
the Glamorgan-Gwent Archaeological Trust (GGAT) entered into discussion
with Debenham Tewson, agents for the site owners B P Properties Ltd, on the
possible archaeological implications of redevelopment. The Trust, in its
capacity as archaeological advisor to the Vale of Glamorgan Borough Council
(VGBC), informed the local planning authority of the archaeological
sensitivity of the site, and also kept in close contact with the Llandough
Community Council, who initially contacted the Trust about the
redevelopment.

The seventeenth century phase is associated with the
Vaughan Family, the nineteenth century work with the Bute Estate. A number
of architectural features were recorded in situ in 1974, (Fig 2) and others
were observed in the rubble following demolition. Sherds of twelfth to
fourteenth century pottery were recovered by E J Beare in 1962-3 on the
slope immediately north-east of the Great House, while it is likely that
some medieval ironwork which is held by the National Museum of Wales was
discovered under the rear wing c 1880 (RCAHMW 1988).

In addition to the recorded evidence noted above there was potential for
further archaeological interest. The possibility that deposits relating to
the early monastic settlement might survive in this area could not be ruled
out, and it was possible that the churchyard may formerly have been more
extensive. Although the main focus of Iron Age and Roman activity appeared
to lie to the south it was possible that further evidence might have
survived in this area. A medieval well, Ffynnon y Drindod, was originally
lecated to the north of the church, and although its exact position is
unknown it may have been located within the development site (RCAHMW 1988).

4) ARCHAEOLOGICAL FIELDWORK: OBJECTIVES AND METHODOLOGY
The aim of the archaeological fieldwork was two-fold. In the first

instance the investigation was aimed at establishing whether deposits
relating to any of the features described in 3 above ‘or indeed any other
type of archaeological activity) were present. The second aim was to
assess the likely impact of redevelopment on the identified archaeological
interests and to recommend to B P Properties methods by which these
interests could best be protected. In addition to the fieldwork other
relevant sources (eg, cartographic) would be consulted as appropriate.

In view of the size and character of the development site it was decided
that the most appropriate way to test the archaeological potential of the
site was by a series of machine cut trenches, the position of which was
determined either by the likelihood of archaeological deposits being
encountered (eg, near the churchyard wall), or at random. It was agreed
that the trenches should mostly be cut to the level of the substrate, but
in some areas this proved impractical. The machine trenches were closely
observed and subject to basic archaeological recording.

The system of machine trenching was in effect a compromise between a more
extensive assessment of the archaeological potential of the site, and the
pre-existing state of knowledge, which in this case was insufficient to
enable informed archaeological decisions to be taken. The results
therefore give a much more reliable indication of the likely archaeological
implications of redevelopment, yet by their nature cannot provide total
assurance on the presence and value of archaeological deposits across the
development site as a whole.

Our integration

An undated GGAT assessment of Great House Farmhouse, filed as August 1990, sent by Cadw with the 1988 survey photographs. The covering email confirms that the 1988 listing assessment did not recommend the house, and that a search of paper files was continuing.

Why it matters: it is one of the few documents on file written after the demolition that describes the site. It is the closest thing so far to the archaeological record of what was there.

What cuts against the case: an assessment made after demolition can only record what was left. Its date also places it after the 1988 decision, so it cannot show what that decision was based on.
