HistoryPoints.org: Wales' QR-plaque heritage network as a free discovery door

Date: 2026-10-30 · Status: research only, nothing sent · Owner: bizdev / site flywheels

What it is (read live 30 Oct 2026)

  • HistoryPoints.org (founded Conwy, Jan 2012) puts small QR plaques on windows, fences, doors, walls, gateposts and noticeboards. Each one opens a short, mapped web page (a "HiPoint") about the spot. Homepage: more than 2,500 places across Wales. About page: 1,400+ by 2018.
  • Wales Coast Path: the path's official site describes it as "believed to be the first QR-code tour of its kind on any long-distance trail in the world". There are about 600 coded locations from Queensferry to Chepstow, with next/previous navigation along the path and pronunciation of Welsh place names.
  • Who writes the pages: mostly unpaid volunteers. Local history societies, museums, trusts, councils and academics are listed as "contributors". Vale of Glamorgan credits: Barry (Merchant Navy Association Wales, Welsh Place-Name Society), Cowbridge History Society, Llantwit Major History Society, Penarth (three named individuals), and Dinas Powys (one local researcher).
  • The credit-line pattern (Dinas Powys war memorial page, read): "We are grateful to for allowing us to summarise here his detailed research…", followed by the title of his booklet Lest We Forget (2014) and how to get it. A researcher's published work gets a named, permanent mention on a page that a physical code points to.
  • No marketing pages. The commissioned-pages page: "we feature only places which are genuinely interesting… you won't find any HiPoint pages which are designed as commercial marketing." Commissions (part-funding of time and costs) come mainly from community, town and county councils, Cadw/Cadwyn and civic societies.
  • Pages are bilingual or more. Most are in at least two languages (volunteer linguists, Welsh icon top right). There are also audio readings and BSL clips.
  • Many readers are not on site. The About page: "Many of our visitors access our website at home, rather than by scanning". So a page earns web readers as well as passers-by.
  • The editor's own account (Rhodri Clark, BBC Nov 2016, Llandrindod Wells 2.3-mile WW1 trail): the trail "would not have been possible without the enthusiastic response we received from many local people, including historians, relatives… and owners of the featured properties." Owner consent is part of every plaque.
  • How to get involved: suggestions, new places and new contributor groups all go through the site's Feedback form (homepage, "Any suggestions?"). The site lists no price for a volunteer page.
  • What isn't known: scan counts and page-view numbers are not published (none found). The commissioned price per plaque is not published.

How to use it (playbook)

  1. Contribute research, not adverts. Offer a summary for a public, genuinely interesting spot, credited to the source: a society, a booklet, or a named researcher who agrees to be named. Expect the credit line plus a note on where the full work can be read, as on the Dinas Powys page. Don't expect a sales link.
  2. Pick a public, owner-friendly site. A church, bridge, war memorial, school or pub, where the owner can sign off a small plaque. Never a contested or private holding.
  3. Write it the house way. About 250–500 words, plain, dated facts, a postcode for the map, and the Welsh place-name meaning (the Welsh Place-Name Society contributes these). Supply the Welsh version too, so the page launches bilingual.
  4. Link up nearby pages. HiPoints carry "next place" navigation, so a page sitting between Penarth and Dinas Powys can join an existing local tour.
  5. Measure honestly. HistoryPoints publishes no counts. Track only what we control: referral visits from historypoints.org in Cloudflare/Search Console, and ?from=historypoints if a credited link is allowed. The general QR caution in qr-codes-print-placement-own-redirects-honest-counts.md applies (exhibition QR uptake is often 8% or less).
