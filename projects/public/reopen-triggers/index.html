Prospective fraud triggers to reopen the case (autoresearch, updated 2026-10-30T00:00Z)

The record is described as it reads; where it may support a ground, that is said as a possibility. No living private individual is said to have acted dishonestly. Conduct by companies and officials is described only by what the documents show.

Test used: setting aside a judgment for fraud, per Takhar v Gracefield Developments [2019] UKSC 13, para 56, adopting RBS v Highland Financial Partners [2013] EWCA Civ 328, para 106. That needs (a) conscious and deliberate dishonesty in evidence given, a statement made or a matter concealed; (b) that this was material, meaning “an operative cause of the court’s decision”, judged “by reference to its impact on the evidence supporting the original decision”; (c) fresh evidence (no reasonable-diligence bar where fraud was not raised at trial: Takhar para 54); and (d) credible evidence that would probably have had an important influence. Each limb is scored 0–5. Court-readiness bar: 4 or more on every limb. Usual route for a Court of Appeal judgment: a fresh claim to set it aside. CPR 52.30 reopening needs “no alternative effective remedy”. Counsel must confirm.

Status: No ground meets the bar yet. They are ranked by how far each is from it (points short across the four limbs).

R3: detail

  • (a) Conscious and deliberate dishonesty: 2/5. HM Land Registry 30 Jul 2026: WA240304 "(11) Form 1C application form from Linklaters & Paines", "(10) Copy of Register edition date 23 February 1983", "(7) Application form D23 (inside exam) and letter dated 8 April 1983". Form 1C (Co.) is titled 'Application for Registration of Freehold Land on Behalf of a Company ... Not on a Recent Purchase - with Statutory Declaration in Support' (HMSO 1967 bibliographic record; the 1983 text not seen). [1987] EWCA Civ 2: the 1974 action "has never been finally heard". HM Land Registry, 25 May 2026: "did not receive or were aware of any interest ... unaware of any dispute" (H05). Run 23 (E114/E116): LRR 1925 r.31 required a Gazette advertisement before absolute first registration and r.33 excused only original lessees and recent purchasers (Ruoff, Conveyancer 1960; r.33 still printed in 1982 and 1989 statute books, snippets); Form 1C (Co.) is the 'not following a recent purchase' route. No Gazette notice naming Llandough Nov 1982-Dec 1983 (the weekly 'about to be registered' series ran to at least 23 May 1977, LG 47219; run 41 E196 corrects cycle 33's 'c. May 1975'; and it was NOT a practice-wide stop: District Land Registries still advertised selected first-registration applications for farmland in 1984-85, e.g. LG 49705, 12 Apr 1984, Birkenhead DLR, Hillcrest Farm and parts of three others, 225 acres, objections invited; so the missing notice for WA240304 was a case decision to dispense with advertisement, which makes the Form 1C (Co.) declaration, SAR item 11, more decisive). Registry procedure, not party dishonesty: no score change.
  • (b) Material: an operative cause: 1/5. [1987] EWCA Civ 2: if she had acquired title by then, "she would have had an overriding interest to which the registration of BP Properties Ltd was necessarily subject". The registration date was not why she lost.
  • (c) Fresh evidence (no diligence bar, Takhar para 54): 3/5. HMLR's 2026 schedule and complaint replies are new.
  • (d) Credible; would probably have had an important influence: 2/5. An official anomaly, but the declaration itself has not been read.
  • What cuts against it: On BP's own case she was its licensee, so a declarant could honestly treat her occupation as BP's possession. The court's words about November 1982 match the register as merged on 2 Feb 1987 (cycle 3 audit). Better routes than reopening are the Land Registration Act 2002 Sch 4 / Sch 8 and, if a declaration proves false, the Perjury Act 1911 s.5, but each still runs into the 1987 judgment. [1987] EWCA Civ 2 opens: BP 'have the paper title to the farm, and indeed since November 1982 they have been the registered proprietors'. Paper title was never in issue (the defence was adverse possession only), so the registration did not found the court's title premise (E26). Run 8 (E49): the 'small window' point fails in law. Land Registration Act 1925 s.70(1)(f) made rights 'acquired or in course of being acquired under the Limitation Acts' overriding and (g) protected persons in actual occupation; s.82 rectification has no time limit (including s.82(1)(d) fraud) and s.82(3) gives no protection against an overriding interest. Her death on 26 Mar 1983 closed no challenge period; the registration could not have cost her the case. Run 28 (E140): the applicant for the 2 Feb 1987 amalgamation is not on any record we hold; the request may date from the 1983 application or be a Registry step, so 'BP amalgamated during the appeal' is not established. Run 41 (E196): the 1984-85 Gazette advertisements are rare (two found by OCR search, both Birkenhead DLR), so dispensing with advertisement was the norm and its absence for WA240304 is not by itself evidence that the Registry was misled; the Wales office's own practice is not read. Run 42 (E199): read now as far as the free record allows: six London Gazette searches 1979-87 (177 page PDFs, pdftotext) found NO first-registration advertisement from the Swansea (Wales) District Land Registry, only the two Birkenhead notices; and Thomson's Dictionary of Banking (1965 and 1985 editions, archive.org full-text snippets, secondary) puts first registration with absolute title as needing 'in some cases, advertisement in the London Gazette and a local paper'. So the missing notice for WA240304 matches the Wales office's apparent practice and is not an anomaly of this case; the run-41 phrase 'a case decision to dispense with advertisement' should be read as routine, not exceptional. The Form 1C (Co.) declaration (SAR item 11) remains the only test.

  • (a) Conscious and deliberate dishonesty: 1/5. None yet. Dishonesty would need BP to have held a written rejection while its evidence or case said there was none.
  • (b) Material: an operative cause: 5/5. So non-acceptance was argued in 1987; only a written rejection sent to BP would be fresh.

The argument that she never accepted the letters was made in 1987 and lost. Public statements are not the same as telling BP. The Court of Appeal judgment holds no acknowledgment of any rejection to BP Pension Trust; it records that "Mrs Buckler therefore never expressly accepted or rejected the terms of the two letters", and that the county court on 4 Nov 1974 stayed execution "pending Mrs Buckler's agreement to the proposals" before BP's solicitors withdrew the warrant (run 2, E23). Mary (Nancy) Williams's typed statement for the 1974 action (wiki 'Mary Williams statement', undated, probably 1974) names the 1974 defendants but mentions none of the Sept 1974 leave, the warrant, the press campaign, BP Properties or the letters, so it most likely predates 31 Oct 1974 and is not a post-letter repudiation (E27). William Rees's Western Mail letter of 19 Nov 1974 is a historian's heritage plea, not an assertion by her. Smith v Molyneaux [2016] UKPC 35 paras 28-30 (read 21 Oct 2026, run 6 E41): the Board applied Buckler, which the parties accepted, and quoted Dillon LJ's "in the absence of any repudiation by her" passage. Persuasive, not binding in England and Wales; it leaves the law as the 1987 court stated it, so only fresh-evidence (Takhar) routes remain. R1: detail

  • (a) Conscious and deliberate dishonesty: 1/5. No document yet shows anyone knew the 'agreed to buy' words were untrue. Record: BP Pension Trust letter of 31 Oct 1974 (quoted in the judgment): "we have found such a purchaser in BP Properties Ltd, who have agreed to buy the property from the Trust". BP Properties letter, same date: "You will be hearing separately from our Pension Trust that they are selling Great House Farm to BP Properties Ltd."
  • (b) Material: an operative cause: 3/5. BP Properties Ltd v Buckler [1987] EWCA Civ 2 (Dillon LJ), on argument 3: "the letters record sufficiently that BP Properties Ltd had agreed to buy the property from the Pension Trust, and BP Properties Ltd was therefore prima facie in a position to grant a licence to Mrs Buckler." The whole result then rests on BP Properties' licence: "from the time of her receipt of the letters, Mrs Buckler was in possession ... by the licence of BP Properties Ltd".
  • (c) Fresh evidence (no diligence bar, Takhar para 54): 2/5. HM Land Registry letter of 30 July 2026 (ref N610CGV, S. G. Haste), WA240304 schedule: "(1) Plan to Conveyance dated 23 May 1975 ... (12) Conveyance 23 May 1975 (13) Plan to conveyance dated 23 February 1975". But the May 1975 completion was before the Court of Appeal: [1987] EWCA Civ 2 records the third argument as deriving "from the fact that the actual conveyance of the farmhouse and garden by BP Pension Trust Ltd to BP Properties Ltd did not take place until May 1975". So only the 23 Feb 1975 plan and the conveyance's recitals (unread) could be fresh. A plan prepared between an agreement and completion is ordinary conveyancing and does not by itself show that no agreement existed on 31 Oct 1974 (run 2, E22: down from 3).
  • (d) Credible; would probably have had an important influence: 1/5. Catalogue entries only; the conveyance itself (and any recital of the contract date) has not been read.
  • What cuts against it: The judge called it "this rather technical point". BP Pension Trust (the legal owner) wrote at the same time and plainly agreed, so a court may treat the licence as given with the owner's authority. An agreement inside the BP group need not have been written to be honest. Item 13 may be a typing slip (wiki A-071). Fraud must be shown to have been an operative cause, judged against the evidence the court relied on. The Court of Appeal already knew the conveyance was not completed until May 1975 and still held that the letters' words were enough. Law of Property Act 1925 s.40(1) (as enacted, read 20 Oct 2026) bars only an ACTION on an unwritten land contract; it does not make an oral agreement void. So an oral intra-group agreement to buy on 31 Oct 1974, written up as a conveyance in 1975, is lawful and does not by itself show the 'agreed to buy' words were untrue (E28). Run 25 (E124): a 1972 directory lists one G W Searle as an officer of both B P Pension Trust Ltd and B P Properties Ltd (OCR snippet, unverified role), so an intra-group agreement recorded only internally is plausible. Run 29 (E144): the 1989 Commission decision (European Commission of Human Rights, admissibility decision, Application No. 14464/88, Buckler v United Kingdom, 14 April 1989 (wiki upload wp-1790112811838.pdf, read in full run 29, E144)) records that the applicant complained the trial judge 'failed to distinguish between the current and previous holders of title to the property': the title-holder point was argued in the litigation itself, so new material on it goes to an issue already decided (finality weighs against reopening unless the material shows dishonesty, limb a).
