Test used and status

No ground meets the court-readiness bar

The record is described as it reads; where it may support a ground, that is said as a possibility. No living private individual is said to have acted dishonestly. Conduct by companies and officials is described only by what the documents show.

Test used: setting aside a judgment for dishonesty in the evidence, per Takhar v Gracefield Developments [2019] UKSC 13, para 56, adopting RBS v Highland Financial Partners [2013] EWCA Civ 328, para 106. That needs (a) conscious and deliberate dishonesty in evidence given, a statement made or a matter concealed; (b) that this was material, meaning “an operative cause of the court’s decision”, judged “by reference to its impact on the evidence supporting the original decision”; (c) fresh evidence (no reasonable-diligence bar where dishonesty was not raised at trial: Takhar para 54); and (d) credible evidence that would probably have had an important influence. Each limb is scored 0–5. Court-readiness bar: 4 or more on every limb. Usual route for a Court of Appeal judgment: a fresh claim to set it aside. CPR 52.30 reopening needs “no alternative effective remedy”. Counsel must confirm.

Status: No ground meets the bar yet. They are ranked by how far each is from it (points short across the four limbs).

R1 in detail

Scores, record, what cuts against it, next record
  • (a) Conscious and deliberate dishonesty: 1/5. No document yet shows anyone knew the ‘agreed to buy’ words were untrue. Record: BP Pension Trust letter of 31 Oct 1974 (quoted in the judgment): “we have found such a purchaser in BP Properties Ltd, who have agreed to buy the property from the Trust”. BP Properties letter, same date: “You will be hearing separately from our Pension Trust that they are selling Great House Farm to BP Properties Ltd.”
  • (b) Material: an operative cause: 3/5. BP Properties Ltd v Buckler [1987] EWCA Civ 2 (Dillon LJ), on argument 3: “the letters record sufficiently that BP Properties Ltd had agreed to buy the property from the Pension Trust, and BP Properties Ltd was therefore prima facie in a position to grant a licence to Mrs Buckler.” The whole result then rests on BP Properties’ licence: “from the time of her receipt of the letters, Mrs Buckler was in possession … by the licence of BP Properties Ltd”.
  • (c) Fresh evidence (no diligence bar, Takhar para 54): 3/5. HM Land Registry letter of 30 July 2026 (ref N610CGV, S. G. Haste), WA240304 schedule: “(1) Plan to Conveyance dated 23 May 1975 … (12) Conveyance 23 May 1975 (13) Plan to conveyance dated 23 February 1975”. Disclosed in 2026; whether the 1975 conveyance and its recitals were before Hollis J or the Court of Appeal is unknown.
  • (d) Credible; would probably have had an important influence: 1/5. Catalogue entries only; the conveyance itself (and any recital of the contract date) has not been read.
  • What cuts against it: The judge called it “this rather technical point”. BP Pension Trust (the legal owner) wrote at the same time and plainly agreed, so a court may treat the licence as given with the owner’s authority. An agreement inside the BP group need not have been written to be honest. Item 13 may be a typing slip (wiki A-071). Dishonesty must be shown to have been an operative cause, judged against the evidence the court relied on.
