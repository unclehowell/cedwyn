Reverse genealogy: find the descendants and invite them home (ancestral / roots tourism)

Topic: ancestral (roots) tourism for a place-based history project, run the Ireland Reaching Out way: instead of waiting for descendants to find the place, volunteers trace who left and reach out to the living descendants. Distinct from diaspora networks (GlobalWelsh, NINNAU/WNAA, Australia/NZ societies: those are channels); this is the method that produces named, warm descendants and a reason to visit or buy. Harvested 2026-10-31 ~23:15Z. "ancestral", "roots tour", "reverse genealogy", "Ireland Reaching Out" had 0 hits in INDEX.md before this.

The evidence (read, with sources)

  • Ireland Reaching Out (Ireland XO), founded 2009 by Mike Feerick as a pilot in South-East Galway (44,000 people, about 1% of Ireland). Irish America, Aug/Sep 2011 (read):
  • "More than 500 volunteers from all of the parishes researched the history, sent letters and reached out to people."
  • Year 1 "Week of Welcomes" (26 Jun-3 Jul 2011): 30 people came, from America, Australia, New Zealand, Argentina and Britain.
  • 200+ already signed up for the next year's Week of Welcomes; national roll-out with Failte Ireland support followed.
  • Honest read: 500 volunteers -> 30 visitors in year one. The method is slow to start and compounds; it is not a launch-week tactic.
  • Wikipedia (read, secondary): national "reverse genealogy" programme launched 14 Mar 2012; by Mar 2013 at least 2,000 volunteers in all 32 counties, active in 800+ civil parishes; now 100,000+ members, 32 volunteer networks. A GAA partnership (Dec 2011) linked it to 500 clubs worldwide. In 2015 it mapped houses so people could see who had lived in them.
  • VisitScotland Business Support, "Ancestral tourism" (updated 25 Feb 2025, read): at least 40 million people claim Scottish ancestry; in the 2023 Scotland Visitor Survey an average 41% of USA/Canada/Australia respondents cited ancestry as a major motivation for the trip. Ancestral visitors "stay longer", are "more likely to visit outside the peak summer months of July and August", and are "likely to become repeat visitors". Tips: know the surnames tied to your area and their burial places/monuments; tell the story of an old building with photographs of its origins; point visitors to the local record office and library; list local events where they can meet the community.
  • Scottish Council on Archives press release, 16 Mar 2022 (read): ancestral tourism "worth over GBP 100m a year to the Scottish economy ... per research commissioned by VisitScotland"; ancestral visitors "stay longer and spend more". (The underlying VisitScotland study itself: not checked.)
  • Business Gateway market report, Feb 2026 (read): repeats VisitScotland Travel Trends 2026 (40 million) and notes older domestic tourists' interest in family history; 44% of domestic holidaymakers named "visiting places of historical interest" as the most appealing activity (Mintel/VisitBritain via that report; primary not checked).

How to do it (the playbook)

  1. Build the "who left" list from records you already hold. For each surname/household tied to the place: who, when they left, where to (census gaps, emigration lists, chapel records, newspapers). One row per emigrant with a source ID.
  2. Trace forward to living descendants only through public, consented channels: public family trees (FamilySearch, WikiTree), one-name studies, surname message boards, diaspora society notices. Never cold-contact a private person found by data-matching.
  3. Publish an ancestor page per family/surname ("Families of the parish: Williams, ...") with sources and a "Is this your family? Tell us" form. This turns reverse genealogy into inbound: descendants searching their surname + place land on it.
  4. Give them a dated reason to come or connect: a named welcome week, a guided walk, a reading of the records online for those who cannot travel. Ireland XO's year-one 30 visitors came to a fixed week, not an open invitation.
  5. Count honestly: members/sign-ups on the families pages, "this is my family" submissions, visits booked, e-books bought by those contacts. Expect year one small (30 from 500 volunteers) and judge on year-two sign-ups (200+).
  6. Make them part of it: descendants supply photos, letters and family memory, which enlarges the archive (and the next edition of the e-book). Ireland XO's "they are our relatives" framing is the tone.

How we apply it to Cedwyn

  • Fits the owner's chronology rules: the Williams family at Great House Farm / Ty Mawr and other Llandough/Leckwith households are exactly the "who left" list. Start a Families of Llandough register (internal first) from the evidence catalogue: emigrant, date, destination, source ID. Patagonia (Argentina) is a natural destination column, matching the Argentina hub and the Ireland XO year-one mix.
  • Public page (PROPOSAL, owner's GO): a neutral "Families of the parish" index under /research/ with a "Is this your family? Share what you hold" form feeding HubSpot as a draft task. Editorial rules apply: never name the owner, no family holdings or addresses of living people, no banned words, no insignia, no likeness. Living descendants are never named publicly without their written consent.
  • Outreach stays inbound or society-mediated: notices in diaspora channels already mapped (NINNAU/WNAA, GlobalWelsh, Australia/NZ societies, Welsh Patagonia groups) asking descendants of named emigrants to get in touch. Each notice is drafted as a [BIZDEV] HubSpot task and waits for GO; a harvest run sends nothing.
  • Dated reason: tie it to the guided Llandough walk and the St David's fortnight (Wales Week files) as a "Welcome home walk"; for those abroad, a live reading of the records. Measure sign-ups per family page and "this is my family" submissions; book/e-book sales from those contacts tagged with utm_campaign=families.
  • Expect small numbers first (Ireland XO: 30 visitors in year one) and do not report it as failure before the second cycle.
