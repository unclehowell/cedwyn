Community archaeology dig days and test-pit weekends (the CAER / Access Cambridge model)

Harvested 2026-11-01 (skills harvest ~02:27Z). Sources at the foot. Figures marked (snippet) come from search previews I did not open in full.

What it is

A community dig turns residents into participants. People dig 1 m x 1 m test pits in their own gardens or on a public green, or they join a month-long supervised excavation. The audience gets made during the dig: visitors, local press, school groups, and a mailing list of people who now have a personal stake in the site's story.

Evidence (practitioner numbers)

  • CAER Heritage Project, Caerau & Ely, Cardiff (Cardiff University + Action in Caerau and Ely + schools, since 2011). Season 3 at Caerau Hillfort (July 2015): more than 200 volunteers worked beside the university team during a month-long dig, and 2,000 visitors came. The finds (a Neolithic causewayed enclosure, a large pottery group, a Roman brooch) made BBC Wales news as "Cardiff is 6,000 years old" (BBC, read).
  • CAER later dug test pits in residents' gardens (Cardiff University news, 2020, "socially-distanced dig": a one-square-metre pit per household, results shared back; page 403 here, snippet). It also dug at Trelai Park playing fields, where a Bronze Age burial site was found and coverage reached India and the US. Facebook post (snippet): "over 100 local volunteers, including school-children and students".
  • CAER's assets are now a Lottery-funded Hidden Hillfort heritage centre plus trails, films, accredited courses and VR. In other words, the dig was the start of a whole local heritage product line (ACE page, read).
  • Access Cambridge Archaeology, Wendens Ambo (Aug 2018, read): 10 test pits over one weekend, 70+ residents of all ages, 3 archaeologists, the village hall as base, Heritage Lottery money. A local society found the pit sites and ran the paperwork. Commemorative water bottles. A pit on the recreation ground drew passers-by: "everyone walking by taking an interest". Teen volunteers collected feedback.
  • ACA tag page (snippet): 86% said there was nothing they did not enjoy.
  • Lewis 2022 (snippet): around 350 residents dug 80 test pits, with measured social outcomes.
  • National Trust "Big Dig" (Changing Chalk, Sussex; snippet): over 10,000 residents, school children and volunteers in coordinated test pits.

How to run one (steps)

  1. Partner first. Work with a university outreach team, the regional trust (in Wales: Heneb, the Trust for Welsh Archaeology) or a unit like ACA. They bring the archaeologists, recording sheets and finds handling. Never dig without one.
  2. Permissions. Get each landowner's written OK for each pit. Scheduled monuments need consent; don't touch them. Any finds that may be treasure follow the Portable Antiquities Scheme (PAS Cymru in Wales).
  3. Format. Run a weekend of 1 m test pits, 8–12 pits, in gardens and one public green so it can be seen. Brief everyone in the morning, take the group photo, and run a finds table in the hall.
  4. Content capture. Run a daily dig diary with a photo of every notable find and a "what we know now" map. Each find becomes a post. Capture short vertical clips of the moment something is lifted.
  5. Funnel. Use a sign-up sheet with email consent at the hall, a QR code to the project page, and a free results booklet or webpage to follow ("the village's map, rewritten"). Results night = second event, second press story.
  6. Measure. Count participants, visitors, sign-ups, press pieces, and post reach per find. CAER's 200 volunteers to 2,000 visitors is a 10x ratio worth benchmarking against.
