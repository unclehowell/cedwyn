Prospective fraud triggers to reopen the case (autoresearch, updated 2026-10-19T22:00Z)

Our case, prospective only. Not a finding of any court. The record is described as it reads; where it may support a ground, that is said as a possibility. No living private individual is said to have acted dishonestly. Conduct by companies and officials is described only by what the documents show.

Learn more

Test used: setting aside a judgment for fraud, per Takhar v Gracefield Developments [2019] UKSC 13, para 56, adopting RBS v Highland Financial Partners [2013] EWCA Civ 328, para 106. That needs (a) conscious and deliberate dishonesty in evidence given, a statement made or a matter concealed; (b) that this was material, meaning “an operative cause of the court’s decision”, judged “by reference to its impact on the evidence supporting the original decision”; (c) fresh evidence (no reasonable-diligence bar where fraud was not raised at trial: Takhar para 54); and (d) credible evidence that would probably have had an important influence. Each limb is scored 0–5. Court-readiness bar: 4 or more on every limb. Usual route for a Court of Appeal judgment: a fresh claim to set it aside. CPR 52.30 reopening needs “no alternative effective remedy”. Counsel must confirm.

Status: No ground meets the bar yet. They are ranked by how far each is from it (points short across the four limbs).

R1: detail

  • (a) Conscious and deliberate dishonesty: 1/5. No document yet shows anyone knew the ‘agreed to buy’ words were untrue. Record: BP Pension Trust letter of 31 Oct 1974 (quoted in the judgment): “we have found such a purchaser in BP Properties Ltd, who have agreed to buy the property from the Trust”. BP Properties letter, same date: “You will be hearing separately from our Pension Trust that they are selling Great House Farm to BP Properties Ltd.”
  • (b) Material: an operative cause: 3/5. BP Properties Ltd v Buckler [1987] EWCA Civ 2 (Dillon LJ), on argument 3: “the letters record sufficiently that BP Properties Ltd had agreed to buy the property from the Pension Trust, and BP Properties Ltd was therefore prima facie in a position to grant a licence to Mrs Buckler.” The whole result then rests on BP Properties’ licence: “from the time of her receipt of the letters, Mrs Buckler was in possession … by the licence of BP Properties Ltd”.
  • (c) Fresh evidence (no diligence bar, Takhar para 54): 3/5. HM Land Registry letter of 30 July 2026 (ref N610CGV, S. G. Haste), WA240304 schedule: “(1) Plan to Conveyance dated 23 May 1975 … (12) Conveyance 23 May 1975 (13) Plan to conveyance dated 23 February 1975”. Disclosed in 2026; whether the 1975 conveyance and its recitals were before Hollis J or the Court of Appeal is unknown.
  • (d) Credible; would probably have had an important influence: 1/5. Catalogue entries only; the conveyance itself (and any recital of the contract date) has not been read.
  • What cuts against it: The judge called it “this rather technical point”. BP Pension Trust (the legal owner) wrote at the same time and plainly agreed, so a court may treat the licence as given with the owner’s authority. An agreement inside the BP group need not have been written to be honest. Item 13 may be a typing slip (wiki A-071). Fraud must be shown to have been an operative cause, judged against the evidence the court relied on.
  • The one record that would most raise the score: WA240304 item 12 (Conveyance 23 May 1975, especially any recital of the date of the contract for sale) and item 13 (the 23 Feb 1975 plan). Free route first: ask for them by name in the HM Land Registry subject-access/EIR email (not yet sent; slotted Tue 20 Oct 09:45), so this is not a repeat request. Fallback: form OC2, £11 each (Sion’s decision; no spending without it).
  • New or known: NEW. Wiki A-081 says BP Properties ‘held only a contract to buy’ and A-071 flags the item-13 date clash. No wiki page, lead or finding tests whether ANY agreement existed on 31 Oct 1974, or ties it to the Court of Appeal’s answer to argument 3.

R3: detail

  • (a) Conscious and deliberate dishonesty: 2/5. HM Land Registry 30 Jul 2026: WA240304 “(11) Form 1C application form from Linklaters & Paines”, “(10) Copy of Register edition date 23 February 1983”, “(7) Application form D23 (inside exam) and letter dated 8 April 1983”. Form 1C (Co.) is titled ‘Application for Registration of Freehold Land on Behalf of a Company … Not on a Recent Purchase – with Statutory Declaration in Support’ (HMSO 1967 bibliographic record; the 1983 text not seen). [1987] EWCA Civ 2: the 1974 action “has never been finally heard”. HM Land Registry, 25 May 2026: “did not receive or were aware of any interest … unaware of any dispute” (H05).
  • (b) Material: an operative cause: 1/5. [1987] EWCA Civ 2: if she had acquired title by then, “she would have had an overriding interest to which the registration of BP Properties Ltd was necessarily subject”. The registration date was not why she lost.
  • (c) Fresh evidence (no diligence bar, Takhar para 54): 3/5. HMLR’s 2026 schedule and complaint replies are new.
  • (d) Credible; would probably have had an important influence: 2/5. An official anomaly, but the declaration itself has not been read.
  • What cuts against it: On BP’s own case she was its licensee, so a declarant could honestly treat her occupation as BP’s possession. The court’s words about November 1982 match the register as merged on 2 Feb 1987 (cycle 3 audit). Better routes than reopening are the Land Registration Act 2002 Sch 4 / Sch 8 and, if a declaration proves false, the Perjury Act 1911 s.5, but each still runs into the 1987 judgment.
  • The one record that would most raise the score: WA240304 item 11 (Form 1C with its statutory declaration) and item 7 (D23 and the 8 Apr 1983 letter). Already requested in the HM Land Registry subject-access/EIR email (due 28 Oct). PENDING: do not re-ask.
  • New or known: KNOWN. H01 (3/5), H03, H05 in the research flywheel; wiki ‘clandestine registration’ pages.

R2: detail

  • (a) Conscious and deliberate dishonesty: 1/5. None yet. Dishonesty would need BP to have held a written rejection while its evidence or case said there was none.
  • (b) Material: an operative cause: 5/5. [1987] EWCA Civ 2: “It may be that the result would have been different if Mrs Buckler had, as soon as she learned of the letters, plainly told BP Properties Ltd that she did not accept the letters, and maintained her claims to be already the owner of the property; she did not, however, do that.” Also: “in the absence of any repudiation by her of the two letters”; “Mrs Buckler therefore never expressly accepted or rejected the terms of the two letters.”
  • (c) Fresh evidence (no diligence bar, Takhar para 54): 1/5. The cutting cited in 2026 correspondence as a ‘1978 article’ (image ‘newspaper_open_day.jpg’) carries the handwritten date “D. Telegraph 16 April 74” and a National Monuments Record (RCAHMW) stamp. It is the Daily Telegraph of 16 April 1974, six months BEFORE the letters (“The ownership of the house has been in dispute for 25 years … my solicitors tell me I have a valid claim to it”). The letter beside it is annotated “WM 19/11/74” (William Rees, ‘Grievous loss’), dating the wiki’s ’19 November 197?’ item to 1974. European Commission of Human Rights, 14464/88 (14 Apr 1989): “The applicant appealed to the Court of Appeal, on the ground, inter alia, that the applicant’s mother had not accepted the licence offered since she regarded herself as the owner.” So non-acceptance was argued in 1987; only a written rejection sent to BP would be fresh.
  • (d) Credible; would probably have had an important influence: 1/5. No post-4 Nov 1974 communication to BP has been found.
  • What cuts against it: The court recorded that she “accepted her solicitors’ advice” to do nothing while the usual 12-year waiting period from the 1962 order ran out; that evidence probably came from her own side. The argument that she never accepted the letters was made in 1987 and lost. Public statements are not the same as telling BP.
  • The one record that would most raise the score: Any letter from Mary (Nancy) Williams or her 1974 solicitors to BP Pension Trust, BP Properties or Linklaters & Paines between 4 Nov 1974 and 26 Mar 1983, or BP’s List of Documents in the 1984-86 action. Free route: a voluntary client-file request to Hugh James (which took over Loosemores, the appellant’s solicitors) by the client or his personal representative. The BP Archive email of Wed 21 Oct already asks whether BP holds a litigation file, so don’t ask BP again.
  • New or known: KNOWN (claim) · NEW (resolves the ‘1978 article’). Sion has argued the rejection since 2025 (wiki ‘Express rejection of the purported licence’). NEW: the ‘1978 article’ is the 16 Apr 1974 Telegraph, which closes that open lead, and the RCAHMW stamp points to an NMR file (grid ref ST 1680 7331 on the card) as a further holder.

Reporting routes (options for Sion to authorise; nothing has been reported): Action Fraud / South Wales Police; HM Land Registry’s property-fraud line; the Information Commissioner (FOIA s.77 applies only where a record was destroyed or concealed after a request was made). A solicitor’s view first is the safer order.
