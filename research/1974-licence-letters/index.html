On 31 October 1974 BP wrote to “Mrs Buckler (Mrs Williams)” saying she could remain in the farmhouse at Great House Farm under licence [PENDING: EV-0050]. The Court of Appeal later held these letters decisive against the family. The letters came at the end of an autumn in which BP started two court routes against Mary and finished neither.

15 April – 3 July 1974: The open day, a new action, and a defence of ownership
Newspaper article Daily Telegraph, 16 April 1974: “‘Open day’ to save ancient Welsh house”, by Godfrey Francis

Cutting held (RCAHMW-stamped copy, annotated “D. Telegraph 16 April 74”); full transcript at GHF-E090-N03
Catalogue: GHF-E024-03 (= GHF-E090-N03)

Location: family archive and web archive image set; RCAHMW NMR 6048944 records the same article

DOCUMENTED FACT (press). On 15 April 1974 Mary, then 60, opened the farmhouse to the public “to show what the nation is losing”, and hundreds came [EV-0048]. She said the former Glamorgan County Council had given the BP Petroleum Pension Trust planning permission for about 13 houses, “But I will refuse to move because not only is this my home it is part of the nation’s heritage.” She added: “The ownership of the house has been in dispute for 25 years but my family has lived here for more than 400 years and my solicitors tell me I have a valid claim to it.” About 1,000 people had signed a petition, and the report says the family had acquired Ty Mawr in about 1667.

Parcel AThe open day, the action, the plea and the statement all concern the farmhouse and the land around it (Parcel A).

Research note. Her plea was a defence of title. Because the hearing was never relisted, no court decided who owned the house parcel. The April 1974 newspaper report, published before the plea, records the same claim of ownership in public; it is not a judgment.

19 September – November 1974: The old order and the withdrawn warrant
Court papers County court application for leave to enforce the 1962 order, September–October 1974

Evidence description: at the same time BP applied for leave to enforce the possession order of 11 December 1962. On 19 September 1974 the county court judge gave leave; the warrant was to issue at once but not be enforced before 31 October. Mary’s solicitors appealed because she had not been given notice. BP’s letters are dated 31 October; after the hearing of 4 November the landlords’ solicitors withdrew the warrant, at a date before 25 November 1974 (Court of Appeal 1987, paras 11, 13 and 16).

Parcel AThe 1962 order covered the farmhouse and garden.

Research note. The 1962 order was not set aside, and the 1974 action was not dismissed. Both were left undecided.
31 October 1974: The letters
Letter BP to “Mrs Buckler (Mrs Williams)”, 31 October 1974

Catalogue: GHF-E024-05

Location: Not yet established from the present record

Evidence description: BP’s letters, dated 31 October 1974, offered Mary a licence to stay in the farmhouse. The press later reported BP’s version of these letters: “In 1974 BP Properties agreed to allow Mrs Buckler to remain in the farmhouse rent free” (South Wales Echo, 29 April 1988; GHF-E090-N15). In 1988 a BP spokesman said she was allowed to live there rent-free “because of her ill health” (GHF-E090-N07).

Parcel AA licence of the farmhouse: the last attempt to make her occupation of the house parcel look permissive.

Research note. She never accepted the licence and continued to assert ownership in writing and in the press.
Analysis: why 1974 matters
Research note. If BP had a clean title to the house parcel, 1974 was the moment to prove it: by asking the court for a declaration of title, or by taking judgment on Mary’s plea. BP did neither. It adjourned the title fight, abandoned the warrant and sent a licence instead. The reasons the family gives for avoiding a trial of title are the open questions about the 1962 order (single-unit framing, jurisdiction, Mary as owner-claimant rather than a tenant holding over: questions about the 1962 order) and the running of time after Mary refused the 1965 offer.
Research note. The letters are treated as the last identified step in a 1949–1974 sequence that repeatedly sought to characterise Mary’s occupation of Parcel A as permissive. That sequence is distinct from proof of the underlying freehold title, and moral fairness is kept distinct from legal effect: what the 1974 proceedings actually decided, and what evidence of Parcel A title was before the court, remain the open questions.

What happened next: the High Court (July 1986) and the Court of Appeal (31 July 1987) held that the letters made her possession permissive from 1974, whether or not she accepted them, so 12 years of adverse possession never ran [EV-0080; PENDING: EV-0074]. The judgment as presently identified did not finally adjudicate the historic Parcel A title in the adjourned 1974 action. Her title documents were not produced because, as Mary (Nancy) Williams’s c.1974 witness statement (Mary (Nancy) Williams testimony) records, they had been taken from the farmhouse in 1950–52.
