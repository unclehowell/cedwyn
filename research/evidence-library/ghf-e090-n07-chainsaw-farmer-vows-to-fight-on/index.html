2. Verbatim transcript

Square brackets mark editorial notes. Spelling and punctuation follow the cutting.

A FARMER who beat-off bailiffs with a chainsaw last night vowed to continue his defiant fight for his Cardiff home. “I am here to stay and I will fight this to the death,” said Mr Bill Buckler after the four-hour showdown. Yesterday’s events began when five bailiffs and around 15 police officers arrived at Great House Farm, Llandough. Mr Buckler blocked the narrow drive with a tractor, locked the farmhouse doors and barred windows. “They smashed the lock on the gate and walked round the tractor,” Mr Buckler said. “Then they started smashing the doors with poleaxes. My children were right behind the door when the axe came through. That’s when I started my chainsaw and pushed it through the door. They backed off then.” His distressed wife, Branwen, later left the farm with the couples’ children, aged two, three and four. Mr Buckler and several friends and tenants remained. The scenes were the latest episode in a battle between the Buckler family and BP Properties.

Mr Buckler said the farm has been in the family for nearly 400 years, and remains adamant he is the owner, despite a court ruling in favour of BP Properties. He said BP had no claim over the farm except for what they had obtained through the courts. “They have got no documents for it. If it’s theirs, why have I never paid a penny in rent for it in my life?” A farm tenant claimed the latest flare-up was brought about by the Cardiff Bay development. “This land would be worth a fortune,” said Mr Christopher Thompson. “Luxury houses could be built up here to overlook Cardiff Bay. The rich would get richer and the poor poorer.” A spokesman for BP PLC in London said the “unpleasant” events at the farm were regretted. He could not say if or when the bailiffs might return. He claimed the farm was owned by BP Properties, which allowed Mr Buckler’s late mother to live in it rent-free because of her ill health. “When Mrs Buckler died in 1983 it became clear her son was not going to leave,” said the spokesman. “We looked at the problem, which was a sticky one, and we asked him to leave, he declined, and in May 1984 we started court proceedings.” Mr Buckler lost the court hearing in Cardiff and did not appeal, the spokesman said. A South Wales Police spokesman emphasised the officers at the scene were present to prevent a breach of the peace, and not to assist bailiffs. [Photo caption: A defiant Mr Bill Buckler . . . “I’m here to stay.”]

Interpretation and wider context

RECONSTRUCTION. Dating. The cutting carries no date. It describes an eviction attempt that failed after four hours, with the family still in the house. The Wiki dates it to the attempt of 29 April 1988, not to the possession of 29 or 30 November 1988, because the family remained in the farmhouse afterwards and the later BNA extracts (N15) report the 29 and 30 April events in similar terms. This corrects the earlier unsourced entry that had placed the chainsaw stand-off in November (1988: Possession and Demolition). The date remains to be confirmed against a dated page.

Two accounts of ownership side by side. The report puts two positions next to each other without resolving them. Mr Buckler says the family are the owners and BP has no documents. BP’s spokesman says BP Properties owned the farm and tolerated Mrs Buckler’s occupation. Neither statement is evidence of title. Title evidence is on the Land Registry Titles and Two Parcels pages. 🕒

What it adds to the record. It is a contemporary report, by a named journalist, that the eviction was resisted physically and that police attended. BP’s spokesman’s account of the 1983 death and the May 1984 proceedings is one source for the start of the possession action. It should be checked against court records and not relied on alone. The photographs show the scene and a person, not who owns the land.

What this does not show. The cutting does not prove who was right about ownership, and it reports Mr Buckler’s account of the bailiffs’ conduct as his account. It does not record any police or court finding on that conduct. A related charge of threatening words and behaviour on 29 April was later withdrawn as out of time (N12). 🕒

Why 1983

INFERENCE · W-01BP waited for her death before suing, in its own words. A BP spokesman quoted in the 1988 press cutting "Chainsaw farmer vows to fight on" (our working note places it in the South Wales Echo of 29 Apr 1988; the catalogue records the newspaper as not yet identified) gave her ill health as the reason she lived there rent-free, and added: "When Mrs Buckler died in 1983 it became clear her son was not going to leave … in May 1984 we started court proceedings." BP's own letter of 31 Oct 1974 had promised that "no proceedings will be commenced until you personally no longer live there". Our reading: her death, not any new fact about title, released BP to sue.

Rests on: EV-0089 · EV-0050 · EV-0080 · EV-0073

Would be confirmed by: BP's internal file for 1983–84 (BP Archive request, reply due by 16 Nov 2026) recording a decision to wait for her death; identification of the newspaper and full page for the cutting.

Would be refuted by: Evidence that BP planned or began proceedings against her during her lifetime, or that the 1984 writ had a different documented trigger.

Status: Open (our main answer to "why 1983")

CUTS AGAINST THE CASE · W-02Cuts against the case: the same timing is fully consistent with BP simply keeping its 1974 promise not to evict her in her lifetime. Waiting for her death is, on this reading, the conduct the letters promised, not concealment. The Court of Appeal itself described the device: a unilateral licence lets an owner who is "not prepared to incur the obloquy of bringing proceedings" keep "his title alive for very many years until it suits him to evict" — a criticism of the device, not a finding of bad faith.

Rests on: EV-0050 · EV-0080 · research log E132

Would be confirmed by: BP correspondence showing the delay was kept for a reason beyond the promise (for example, to let a registration settle before anyone could contest it).

Would be refuted by: BP correspondence showing the delay was only the promise being kept.
