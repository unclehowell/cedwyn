Working Theory — the 1939 Signature, Mountjoy/Bute Title, the 1955 Seizure and the Twelve-Year Pattern

Working theory · Updated 2026-10-29 · Hypothesis under test, not a finding

NEW WORKING THEORY · 29 OCTOBER 2026

The theory being tested is that corporate and public-body dealings, deeds, tenancy papers and registration steps may have been used to establish or preserve a paper claim over land already belonging to and occupied by the Williams family, while the family's occupation was presented as permissive or treated as undisputed.

This is not an ordinary landlord–tenant explanation. The central propositions are the family's asserted rights under the 1877 arrangement, the reported end of rent in 1928, a questioned signature on the 1939 agreement, the Mountjoy/Bute–Cardiff Corporation title sequence, the 1955 taking of land said to include parts of Parcel A, and the repeated date intervals in the twelve-year chart.

Status: hypothesis under active document-by-document test. The alleged signature mismatch and any coordination must be tested against originals; neither is presented here as an established court finding.

The theory in its own terms

The working theory is that the apparent documentary chain may not represent the true history of title and occupation. The Williams family is said to have held a pre-existing interest under the 1877 arrangement, with that arrangement taking effect when quarrying ended and the final rent was paid in or around 1928. The theory is that later parties may have used corporate conveyances, a 1939 agreement bearing a disputed signature, tenancy offers or re-grants, possession proceedings and registration dates to make the family's possession appear permissive or uncontested—and to establish claims in their own names without corresponding physical occupation of the relevant land.

The theory includes the possibility that the date intervals represent a repeated method: choose a start event, put forward a paper instrument or asserted right, and later rely on a transaction, possession event or registration timed around a historic twelve-year period. This is to be tested against the original document, parcel plan, claimed occupation and registration procedure. A date interval is a lead, not proof by itself.

1939 signature

The signature attributed to John Williams is reported by the family to differ from signatures preserved among family heirlooms. Compare originals, not reproductions alone.

Test the exact date and parcel extent of the alleged Cardiff Corporation conveyance against Mountjoy Ltd's incorporation and asserted Bute connection, the 1938 conveyance to WGR, and the 1939 instrument.

Actual occupation

Test whether third parties physically occupied each parcel for any claimed period, or relied on paper instruments and selected dates while the family occupied and asserted ownership.

The signature comparison

The family's reported comparison is that the signature attributed to John Williams on the 1939 agreement does not match signatures kept among family heirlooms. This is a specific authenticity allegation, not a settled finding.

  1. Preserve provenance: photograph the whole heirloom document front and back, with scale and colour reference, and record its custody. Do not mark the original.
  2. Obtain the best 1939 source: request the original agreement, all counterparts, schedules, witnesses, endorsements and file notes.
  3. Compare like with like: use multiple independently authenticated original signatures near the same period; account for differences between originals and copies.
  4. Seek specialist examination: a forensic document examiner can assess pen movement, line quality, retouching, tracing, ink sequence and copy artefacts.
  5. Trace use and effect: establish who produced and relied on the agreement, when, and whether it was used for Parcel A or only Parcel B.

Possible outcomes—including authentic signature, copy distortion, authorised signing, alteration, or non-genuine signature—must be distinguished. A visual mismatch alone does not identify who signed or why.

The corporate / public-body sequence

  • Cardiff Corporation: obtain the conveyance said to transfer land to the Corporation, its plan/schedules, completion and registration dates, and later transfers affecting A1/C. Establish whether it acquired the freehold, a strip, access, woodland rights or another interest.
  • Mountjoy Ltd: obtain its incorporation file, memorandum/articles, subscribers, directors, accounts, property schedule and liquidation/dissolution trail. Incorporation alone proves neither ownership nor parcel inclusion.
  • 1938 transfer to WGR: test whether the instrument conveyed a reversion for Parcel B or included parts of A/A1/C.
  • 1955 process: locate the order, writ, warrant, execution return, plans, instructions, Council correspondence and records identifying the land physically taken.
  • Land Registry: for 1961, 1970, 1982 and 1983, identify the actual application type, legal basis, plans, declarations, named occupiers, notices, recipients, objections and decision notes.

What would strengthen or weaken the theory?

Would materially support it

  • The Corporation plan excludes disputed parcels while later papers treat them as included.
  • Expert examination finds the 1939 signature inconsistent with authenticated John Williams exemplars, without credible file explanation.
  • A third-party application asserts twelve years' physical possession while primary records show family occupation and no corresponding occupation by the applicant.
  • A notice/service file omits an identifiable occupier or records an objection not reflected in the decision.
  • Repeated parcel descriptions, unsupported start dates or cross-references recur across instruments.
  • The 1955 execution includes A1/C without an instrument or legal basis covering that land.

Would weaken or redirect it

  • Original plans and operative clauses show the instruments covered those parcels.
  • The 1939 original and authenticated exemplars support authenticity, or reliable contemporary records explain the signature.
  • Contemporaneous records establish third-party physical possession during the claimed period.
  • The application file shows the legal basis, accurate plans, notice and documented handling of objections.
  • Intervals dissolve when exact dates, separate holdings or unrelated legal tests are corrected.

A result against one proposition does not decide the others. A questioned signature, misdescribed conveyance, over-broad execution and unnotified registration are distinct questions requiring different records.

How to read the new evidence-interpretation notes

The linked primary-record and artefact pages now include short, item-specific assessments of their relevance to this hypothesis. They distinguish (1) direct title instruments and plans, (2) possession, tenancy and knowledge records, (3) court or registration decisions, (4) family testimony and reported events, and (5) maps, heritage records and administrative disclosure material.

Those categories are not interchangeable. A deed must be read for its operative wording and plan; a map may clarify geography but not by itself vest title; a register shows the recorded position but is not proof that every historic entry was correct; a complaint response shows what the institution said in that process; and a family report remains testimony until corroborated. A missing record is a research gap unless further evidence establishes why it is missing.

Start with the Evidence Library and the public-body artefact index. Each note links back here so readers can compare the hypothesis with the primary record, the alternative interpretation and the next test.
